# OpenEscrow MVP — Unresolved decisions

Companion to [`mvp-spec.md`](./mvp-spec.md). Category A below needs **legal review** because the contract encodes an assumption about real-world rules it has no way to actually enforce or verify — none of it is resolved by engineering and all of it remains open. Category B was a list of product/engineering decisions made by default; as of 2026-07-23 all of them have been explicitly reviewed and approved (see the decision log at the top of `mvp-spec.md`), so they're kept here only as a record of what was decided and why, not as open items.

This contract cannot know or enforce anything about real tenancy law. Every item below is a place where the MVP either hardcodes a specific behavior as a stand-in, or explicitly declines to model something. None of it should be read as "solved" — it's "scoped out and named," which is different.

## A. Requires legal review before any non-testnet use

1. **What legally starts the move-out and claim period.** The MVP uses a single fixed timestamp (`claimWindowStart`) chosen by the landlord at proposal and implicitly accepted by the tenant at funding. Real security-deposit law typically ties the claim-period start to an actual event (move-out date, key return, lease termination) that the contract has no way to observe or verify. If the real move-out date differs from the pre-agreed `claimWindowStart` (early move-out, holdover tenancy, eviction, lease renewal), this contract's timeline and the legally operative timeline will diverge, and the contract will not know.

2. **Who may initiate move-out.** Not modeled at all — there is no on-chain "move-out" event, only the fixed date above. If your jurisdiction/product requires either party to be able to trigger or confirm move-out (rather than a date fixed weeks or months in advance), this is a design gap, not an oversight to patch later — it changes the state machine.

3. **Required claim and response periods.** `claimPeriod`, `responsePeriod`, and `arbiterRulingPeriod` are landlord-set, bounded only by MVP sanity constants (proposed: 5 minutes to 365 days), with no jurisdiction-specific minimums or maximums enforced. Many jurisdictions mandate specific windows (e.g. 14, 21, 30, 60 days) for a landlord to return or account for a deposit. The contract does not know or enforce your jurisdiction's number.

4. **Permitted deduction categories.** The contract has no concept of what a claim is *for* — `claimedAmount` and `evidenceURI` are unstructured. Legitimate deduction categories (unpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear, cleaning) versus impermissible ones are entirely a matter for the arbiter's off-chain judgment. There is no on-chain guardrail preventing a claim for something a court would reject outright.

5. **Evidence and itemization requirements.** The contract still has one `evidenceURI` and one
   aggregate amount per claim (overwritten on amendment, with full history preserved only in event
   logs). The current app and printable report capture structured category, description, and amount
   line items off-chain, and require their total to equal the onchain claim amount, but the contract
   cannot enforce or independently recover those line items. No minimum evidence quality is
   enforced. Whether this is sufficient for an arbiter or court remains outside the contract's
   scope.

6. **Arbiter authority and enforceability.** The "arbiter" here is a wallet address the two parties pick; nothing establishes them as a licensed mediator, arbitrator, or any legally recognized dispute-resolution role. Their ruling is enforced *only* to the extent it moves tokens already in the contract — it carries no independent legal weight, isn't binding outside the contract, and there's no vetting of who's allowed to serve as one. If the product intends for arbiter rulings to be legally meaningful (e.g. admissible, or a substitute for small-claims proceedings), that requires a real legal/process framework this spec does not attempt to provide.

7. **Rules for abandoned or unavailable arbiters.** The MVP default (spec §8) is: if the arbiter doesn't rule within `arbiterRulingDeadline`, the entire disputed amount goes to the tenant. This is a strong, tenant-favoring default with no nuance (no partial-fault split, no extension request, no escalation). Confirm this is the intended policy rather than an engineering convenience — see item B4 below, which covers the same question from the product-decision angle.

## B. Product/engineering decisions — approved 2026-07-23

1. **Tenant non-response defaults to full dispute, not acceptance** (spec §6). Chosen because it matches the project's "burden of proof on the claimant" philosophy and because auto-accepting a claim on tenant inaction is exactly the failure mode flagged in the earlier feasibility review of the original design (non-blocking disputes). Approved as-is: every unresponsive-tenant claim requires the arbiter to actually engage, even when the claim is legitimate and the tenant just never opened the notification — that friction is accepted as the cost of never auto-paying the landlord from tenant silence.

2. **Amend-only-downward, capped at exactly one amendment, deadline never resets.** *Revised from the original draft*, which had capped amendments at 3 and reset `responseDeadline` on each one. The approved version is stricter on both axes: at most one amendment ever, and it can never touch the response deadline in either direction. This fully (not just partially) eliminates the amendment-based griefing vector described in the original draft, at the cost of landlords having a single, non-extendable chance to revise a claim.

3. **Acceptance and funding are one atomic action** (`tenantAcceptAndFund`, spec §3/§6), not two separate steps. Approved as-is. Off-chain negotiation and terms review happen before `createAgreement`; once the tenant funds, there is no separate prior "I agree to these terms but haven't paid yet" state.

4. **Arbiter-timeout default favors the tenant fully** (spec §8). Approved as-is, for consistency with the "deposit is tenant's property unless proven otherwise" framing. This remains the single most consequential default value in the spec — worth re-confirming again specifically if the product's target users or market ever shift away from being explicitly tenant-protective.

5. **No admin role of any kind** (spec §10). *Revised from the original draft*, which proposed a deployer-gated `pauseNewAgreements()` circuit breaker. That proposal is withdrawn — the approved contract has zero privileged addresses, full stop. Revisit only if a future non-MVP version needs an incident-response mechanism; that would be a deliberate re-introduction of centralization, not a default to fall back on.

6. **Onchain evidence limited to hash + opaque pointer + type + timestamp + submitter, no raw content** (spec §2, §9, decision 6). Approved as-is. This bounds what the *contract* can leak, but does not and cannot prevent someone from publishing sensitive unencrypted content at the URI a hash points to — that remains a usage risk, not something enforceable in Solidity. Any production frontend must make the "public IPFS is not private" warning impossible to miss before a user uploads anything.

7. **No on-chain negotiation.** The landlord sets every term; the tenant's only on-chain choice is fund-as-proposed or don't. Approved as-is, unchanged from the original draft.

8. **Role identity is wallet-address-only, no KYC/verification of any kind.** Approved as-is for testnet/demo scope, unchanged from the original draft. A production version holding real deposits will need to decide how much (if any) identity binding matters, especially for the arbiter role given item A6 above.

## C. Starting-point analysis for category A (not legal advice)

None of this resolves category A - it's a head start for whoever does the actual legal review, scoped to **US residential tenancy law, generic multi-state patterns** per the project's direction. Security-deposit statutes are set at the **state** level (sometimes city level), vary significantly, and change over time; every specific number below needs to be confirmed against current statute in the actual target state(s) before being relied on. Nothing here is legal advice.

1. **What starts the claim period.** Most states tie it to an observable event - "termination of the tenancy" and/or actual surrender of possession - not a pre-agreed calendar date. The two commonly diverge: a fixed-term lease's stated end date is not always the same moment as when the tenant actually vacates (early move-out, holdover, eviction judgment, mutual early termination). Some states also condition the landlord's return-deadline clock on the *tenant* first providing a forwarding address; a minority treat "no forwarding address" as tolling the deadline indefinitely, while most now default to a reasonable-diligence standard if the landlord otherwise knows where to send it. A production version likely needs an on-chain move-out event (mutual confirmation, or single-party trigger with a contestation window) rather than relying solely on `claimWindowStart` fixed at proposal time. For a testnet MVP, the fixed-date simplification is a reasonable and clearly-flagged stand-in.

2. **Who may initiate move-out.** Two common patterns worth evaluating: (a) tenant-initiated, matching the legal "surrender of possession" concept, with a landlord confirmation/objection window; or (b) automatic at a fixed lease-end date for non-renewing fixed-term leases, which is closer to what this MVP already does. A related, often-overlooked point: several states give the tenant a statutory right to request a **pre-move-out ("initial") inspection**, conducted while the tenant can still remedy issues before final deductions are assessed - if the landlord is required to offer this and doesn't, it can independently bar or reduce their ability to claim certain deductions later, regardless of what the final claim says. Which pattern(s) to support depends on target jurisdictions and lease structures (fixed-term vs. month-to-month) - a production version may need more than one path, plus a way to represent "pre-move-out inspection was offered/conducted" if that matters in-jurisdiction.

3. **Required claim/response periods, and the penalty for missing them.** Concrete reference points, to be confirmed against current statute: many states cluster around 14-30 days for the landlord to return the deposit or provide an itemized accounting, with some in the 45-60 day range and California specifically well-known for 21 days. The number itself is only half the picture - **many states impose statutory damages for non-compliance**, commonly a multiplier of the withheld or entire deposit amount (2x and 3x are both common figures across different states), sometimes plus attorney's fees, awarded to the tenant essentially automatically if the landlord blows the deadline or skips the itemized statement. This is a strong real-world incentive structure this contract doesn't model or surface at all - a production version should probably warn landlords prominently that missing the jurisdiction's deadline can carry real statutory penalties independent of whether their underlying claim was valid. Recommendation unchanged from before: treat these as jurisdiction-configured parameters with sane per-jurisdiction defaults, not a single landlord-chosen global constant - the current MVP's 5-minutes-to-365-days range is a deliberately permissive testnet convenience, not a jurisdiction-compliant default.

4. **Permitted deduction categories.** Commonly permitted: unpaid rent, unpaid utility charges the tenant was responsible for, and the cost to repair damage beyond "ordinary wear and tear" - a real legal term of art (expected deterioration from normal living: minor carpet wear, small nail holes, sun-fading) distinct from damage (large holes, stains, pet damage, broken fixtures). Cleaning costs are often only chargeable if the unit wasn't left "reasonably clean," and several states disallow routine end-of-tenancy cleaning/carpet-cleaning fees regardless of what the lease says. A frequently-missed nuance: many states require **depreciation/useful-life proration** for damaged items rather than full replacement cost - charging a tenant the full price of a brand-new carpet to replace one that was already most of the way through its expected life is often not a valid deduction amount even where the damage itself is chargeable. Commonly *not* permitted at all: ordinary wear and tear, and pre-existing damage (which is exactly why move-in condition documentation matters so much in practice - it's the baseline everything else is measured against, and this contract has no concept of a move-in baseline either). A structured evidence schema (category enum + line-item amounts, ideally with a nod toward depreciation) would better mirror what statutes typically require and give arbiters a clearer framework - worth prioritizing in the next spec revision.

5. **Evidence and itemization sufficiency.** Many states legally *require* an itemized statement, often with receipts, invoices, or good-faith repair estimates attached (sometimes with a further short window to supply receipts if repairs weren't complete yet at the time of the initial statement), as a condition of a deduction being valid at all - "some evidence exists" is a materially lower bar than most statutes set. The single-`uri`-per-claim schema here is a testnet simplification; a production version should move toward structured, itemized evidence (per-line-item hash + category + amount + move-in-baseline reference) to actually reflect statutory itemization requirements rather than just gesturing at them.

6. **Arbiter authority and enforceability.** Two coherent paths, not really a spectrum in between: (a) treat the on-chain claim/evidence/dispute trail purely as an *evidentiary record* feeding into a real small-claims or mediation process - the contract enforces fund movement, the court or licensed mediator provides actual legal weight; or (b) build real binding arbitration on top, which is a substantial legal undertaking, not an engineering one. Path (b) has a specific extra obstacle worth flagging: several states have moved to **limit or invalidate mandatory pre-dispute arbitration clauses in residential leases specifically**, out of concern for the landlord/tenant power imbalance - a clickwrap "you agree the named arbiter's decision is final" buried in a UI flow is exactly the kind of clause that doctrine targets, and its enforceability against a tenant could be limited or void in the jurisdictions that have adopted it, independent of anything this contract does correctly. Small-claims courts, by contrast, already exist precisely to handle low-dollar deposit disputes without requiring an attorney, and are typically the actual venue these disputes end up in today - path (a) rides on infrastructure that already works and doesn't run into the arbitration-clause problem, which is why it remains the lower-risk starting point and aligns with this project's own "legal admissibility pilot" roadmap item.

7. **Abandoned/unavailable arbiter default.** The tenant-favoring timeout default (item A7/B4) is defensible as a matter of product philosophy, but its *enforceability* as a private dispute-resolution outcome likely depends on both parties having given clear, informed, specific consent to that risk allocation at agreement creation - not just having technically clicked through a UI, and (per item 6 above) potentially not enforceable as binding arbitration at all in states that restrict such clauses regardless of consent quality. A production version should make this default explicit and prominent in whatever terms/disclosure flow precedes `createAgreement`, not just documented here - and should not oversell it as legally binding arbitration where the jurisdiction wouldn't recognize it as such.
